Modi Lipi

The Bent Script of Maharashtra's History

Introduction to Modi Lipi

The Modi script (मोडी, 𑘦𑘻𑘚𑘲) is a cursive abugida derived from Devanagari and optimized for speed. Its looping, compact letterforms allowed rapid drafting, making it the primary vehicle for Marathi administrative, commercial, and literary writing for centuries. Designed for continuous strokes rather than segmented lines, Modi enabled efficient record-keeping across the dynamic political and economic systems of western India.

History of Modi Lipi

Proto-Modi forms appear by the 12th century, maturing during the Yadava Dynasty in the 13th century, when figures such as Hemadpant are credited—traditionally though not conclusively—with shaping it into an administrative script.[1] Under the Bahmani Sultanate (14th–16th c.), Modi gained traction as a faster alternative to the more angular and labor-intensive Devanagari styles of the period.

By the 17th century, Shivaji’s administration adopted Modi as its documentary backbone. His chief secretary, Balaji Avaji Chitnis, systematized calligraphic conventions such as the Chitnisi style to maximize legibility and speed.[2] Modi coexisted with Devanagari (Balbodh) until the mid-20th century, when printing limitations under British rule—metal type for Modi was expensive and error-prone—pushed publishers toward Devanagari.[3] Post-independence language policies further accelerated the shift. Although everyday use declined, organized revival efforts since 2014—particularly in Pune—have trained hundreds of new learners.[7] Despite this, only an estimated 200–250 people in Maharashtra today read Modi fluently.[9]

Modi Lipi and the Maratha Confederacy

During the Maratha Empire (1674–1818), Modi was the default script of governance and internal communication. It carried imperial correspondence, diplomatic instructions, military orders, treaties, land grants, revenue accounts, hundi networks, and encrypted messages between Shivaji, the Peshwas, and regional commanders.[4]

Its cursive efficiency was well matched to the confederacy’s expanding administrative geography, enabling rapid preparation of kaulnāmas, patras, and battlefield directives. The earliest dated Modi document—1389—is preserved at the Bharat Itihas Samshodhak Mandal in Pune.[5] Literary forms such as bakhars flourished alongside bureaucratic documentation, and multiple hands (Chitnisi, Bilavalkari, Mahadevapanti) developed for specialized uses. After 1818, British administrative restructuring and the rise of print culture hastened Modi’s decline, but the script remains indispensable for interpreting Maratha-era primary sources.

The Modi Corpus: Scope and Preservation

India’s manuscript heritage—over ~10 million items—includes a substantial body of Modi materials.[6] Current estimates list more than 10,000 surviving Modi works:

  • ~200 bakhars (e.g., Bhausahebanchi Bakhar)
  • several thousand administrative letters, revenue records, and legal documents
  • extensive ledgers and correspondence preserved in public archives

Major repositories include BISM Pune (5,000+ Modi items), the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute (2,000+ Marathi manuscripts), and numerous private family collections, especially those of former Peshwa and Chitnis lineages.

Conversion remains limited: fewer than 5% of Modi manuscripts are fully transliterated into Devanagari due to the script’s cursive ligatures, highly variable handwriting styles, and the shortage of trained readers. Digital initiatives—including machine-learning datasets like MoDeTrans (2,043 labelled samples)—are accelerating transcription, but large segments remain uncatalogued, deteriorating, or inaccessible, risking permanent loss.[10]
